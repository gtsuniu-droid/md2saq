CASE 03: SUDDEN DIZZINESS WHILE PREPARING BREAKFAST
EXPLAINED MODEL ANSWERS AND MARKING GUIDE | Total: 26 marks

MARKING PRINCIPLES
- Apply the explicit part caps. Do not award additional marks for longer lists.
- Award credit for clinically equivalent answers and sensible locally applicable management.
- Do not credit the same fact twice within a part; a repeated diagnosis label can be credited at a later question only when that question explicitly asks for updated diagnosis.
- No negative marking. Unsafe suggestions receive no credit for the affected criterion and should be addressed in feedback.
- These are example answers, not an exhaustive list. Award partial credit for correct components; use the stated caps.
- Where a 1-mark item explicitly asks for an action/test and its reason, normally award 0.5 for each.
- For Question 1, do not require every suggested finding to be present. A normal examination may be compatible with an episodic diagnosis but is rarely diagnostic by itself.

QUESTION 1 (9 marks)

For each of three diagnoses: diagnosis 0.5; mechanism 0.5; each additional history feature 0.5; examination finding 0.5; interpretation 0.5. Total 9.

Award up to 3 marks per distinct, plausible diagnosis using the allocation in the question. Credit equivalent discriminating features and well-justified alternatives. Features must add to the original stem. Do not require every suggested finding to be present, and do not treat its absence as a definitive exclusion.

1. Posterior circulation ischaemic stroke
   Mechanism: Thromboembolic interruption of blood supply affects cerebellar/brainstem balance pathways.
   History 1: Exact onset/last-known-well time and whether symptoms have persisted.
   History 2: Diplopia, dysphagia, dysarthria or prior brief similar neurological episodes.
   Examination: Marked gait/truncal ataxia or a central ocular-motor sign.
   Interpretation: Localises concern to central pathways even with normal limb power.

2. Vestibular neuritis
   Mechanism: Unilateral peripheral vestibular hypofunction produces asymmetric vestibular input.
   History 1: Continuous vertigo over hours to days.
   History 2: Absence of auditory symptoms and a recent infective illness.
   Examination: Coherent peripheral vestibular pattern on expert examination.
   Interpretation: Would support a peripheral alternative, although vascular risk still requires careful assessment.

3. Cerebellar haemorrhage
   Mechanism: Acute bleeding disrupts cerebellar function and may cause mass effect.
   History 1: Severe sudden headache or neck pain.
   History 2: Anticoagulant exposure/adherence, bleeding tendency or recent trauma.
   Examination: Truncal ataxia with deteriorating consciousness or other posterior-fossa signs.
   Interpretation: Raises concern for an intracranial emergency; examination alone cannot reliably distinguish infarction from haemorrhage.

Other acceptable differentials when justified:
- Posterior circulation TIA if the episode has completely resolved
- Arrhythmic presyncope if a brief perfusion-related event rather than ongoing vestibular symptoms is established

QUESTION 2 (5 marks)

a) State the leading diagnosis and explain the significance of the episode three days earlier. [2 marks]
- [1 marks] Acute posterior circulation stroke, most likely ischaemic; haemorrhage still requires exclusion.
- [1 marks] Transient vertigo with diplopia may have been a posterior circulation TIA: symptom resolution does not make it benign.

b) Outline the urgent brain/vascular imaging assessment to discuss with the stroke team, giving its immediate clinical purposes. [2 marks]
- [1 marks] Urgent local stroke imaging pathway: non-contrast CT to identify haemorrhage and CT angiography where indicated to assess large-vessel occlusion.
- [1 marks] MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging may clarify suspected posterior-fossa infarction; do not delay time-critical stroke decisions waiting for MRI. Accept an appropriate rapid MRI-based local pathway.

c) Identify ONE medication-history detail that could change acute treatment eligibility and explain why. [1 marks]
- [1 marks] Exact anticoagulant, last dose and adherence (with relevant renal function) affect bleeding risk and reperfusion decisions; simply saying “on blood thinners” is insufficient.


QUESTION 3 (6 marks)

a) Explain why neither the CT result nor improvement in nausea makes discharge safe. [2 marks]
- [1 marks] Early posterior-fossa ischaemia may not be visible on non-contrast CT.
- [1 marks] Symptom relief does not reverse the persisting central examination findings or establish safe mobility.

b) State FOUR immediate management priorities, including definitive-care assessment and supportive care. [4 marks]
- [1 marks] Activate/continue urgent senior stroke-team care and monitored admission.
- [1 marks] Rapidly assess eligibility for thrombolysis and/or thrombectomy using onset, imaging, anticoagulant exposure and local protocol; no dose recall required.
- [1 marks] Keep nil by mouth until swallowing is screened; protect airway and prevent aspiration as indicated.
- [1 marks] Monitor neurological state/vitals and provide safe hydration, glucose/oxygen management as indicated and falls precautions. Do not lower this BP routinely without a stroke-specific indication.


QUESTION 4 (6 marks)

a) Explain anatomically how a stroke can produce severe dizziness and inability to stand without limb weakness. [2 marks]
- [1 marks] Cerebellar or brainstem vestibular pathways coordinate equilibrium and eye movements.
- [1 marks] A lesion affecting those pathways may spare corticospinal motor pathways, leaving power normal.

b) Explain how atrial fibrillation can cause this presentation and why checking the pulse alone does not establish the diagnosis. [2 marks]
- [1 marks] Atrial thrombus can embolise into cerebral arteries and cause ischaemia.
- [1 marks] An irregular pulse identifies a risk/source, not the location or nature of the neurological lesion; it does not distinguish embolic stroke from other causes.

c) If an early MRI were also reported as negative but the central examination findings persisted, what should happen next, and why? [2 marks]
- [1 marks] Continue specialist stroke/neurological assessment, observation and consideration of repeat or additional imaging.
- [1 marks] Early small posterior circulation infarcts can be missed; imaging must be interpreted alongside the persistent clinical syndrome.


COMMON REASONING ERRORS
- Using normal power, a negative FAST screen or a normal CT to exclude posterior stroke.
- Giving antithrombotics reflexively before haemorrhage and reperfusion decisions are addressed.

CLINICAL THINKING TO TAKE INTO THE NEXT PATIENT
- A transient focal neurological symptom changes the meaning of a dizziness history.
- Normal tests only reassure about the conditions they can reliably detect.
