CASE 02: DIZZINESS AFTER A RECENT RESPIRATORY ILLNESS
EXPLAINED MODEL ANSWERS AND MARKING GUIDE | Total: 25 marks

MARKING PRINCIPLES
- Apply the explicit part caps. Do not award additional marks for longer lists.
- Award credit for clinically equivalent answers and sensible locally applicable management.
- Do not credit the same fact twice within a part; a repeated diagnosis label can be credited at a later question only when that question explicitly asks for updated diagnosis.
- No negative marking. Unsafe suggestions receive no credit for the affected criterion and should be addressed in feedback.
- These are example answers, not an exhaustive list. Award partial credit for correct components; use the stated caps.
- Where a 1-mark item explicitly asks for an action/test and its reason, normally award 0.5 for each.
- For Question 1, do not require every suggested finding to be present. A normal examination may be compatible with an episodic diagnosis but is rarely diagnostic by itself.

QUESTION 1 (9 marks)

For each of three diagnoses: diagnosis 0.5; mechanism 0.5; each additional history feature 0.5; examination finding 0.5; interpretation 0.5. Total 9.

Award up to 3 marks per distinct, plausible diagnosis using the allocation in the question. Credit equivalent discriminating features and well-justified alternatives. Features must add to the original stem. Do not require every suggested finding to be present, and do not treat its absence as a definitive exclusion.

1. Vestibular neuritis
   Mechanism: Acute unilateral vestibular dysfunction causes an imbalance in resting vestibular input.
   History 1: Persistent rather than separate brief episodes.
   History 2: No new hearing loss or tinnitus.
   Examination: Direction-fixed horizontal-torsional nystagmus with an abnormal head impulse on the affected side when assessed by a trained clinician.
   Interpretation: Supports peripheral vestibular hypofunction in the appropriate syndrome, but isolated findings are not sufficient to exclude stroke.

2. Posterior circulation ischaemic stroke
   Mechanism: Ischaemia disrupts central vestibular or cerebellar pathways.
   History 1: Abrupt maximal onset or transient diplopia, dysarthria or dysphagia.
   History 2: Vascular disease, smoking or recent neck injury/pain.
   Examination: Direction-changing gaze-evoked nystagmus or severe truncal/gait ataxia.
   Interpretation: A central sign increases concern despite preserved gross limb power.

3. Labyrinthitis
   Mechanism: Inner-ear involvement affects both vestibular and cochlear function.
   History 1: Acute new hearing loss or tinnitus with the vertigo.
   History 2: Ear pain, discharge or a recent ear infection.
   Examination: Hearing deficit on bedside assessment, with otoscopy for evidence of infection.
   Interpretation: Cochlear involvement favours a process beyond isolated vestibular neuritis; acute hearing loss does not itself rule out stroke.

Other acceptable differentials when justified:
- Vestibular migraine if prior similar episodes and migraine features emerge
- Drug or alcohol toxicity if exposure and examination support it

QUESTION 2 (5 marks)

a) State the most likely diagnosis and explain ONE finding that supports it. [2 marks]
- [1 marks] Left vestibular neuritis / acute unilateral peripheral vestibulopathy.
- [1 marks] Accept unilateral abnormal head impulse, the coherent direction-fixed pattern, or continuous vertigo without auditory involvement, interpreted in the full examination.

b) Explain why the recent cold and normal limb power were insufficient to make this diagnosis at presentation. [2 marks]
- [1 marks] A recent respiratory illness is common and nonspecific; it cannot establish a peripheral cause.
- [1 marks] Posterior circulation stroke may affect balance/eye movements without weakness; a targeted neurological/vestibular examination was still needed.

c) Name ONE circumstance in which urgent further neurological assessment or imaging would still be required. [1 marks]
- [1 marks] A central/equivocal or unreliable examination, new neurological/hearing findings, inability to walk, or unexpected deterioration warrants reassessment and appropriate imaging.


QUESTION 3 (6 marks)

a) Give TWO immediate supportive measures, with a brief purpose for each. [2 marks]
- [1 marks] Short-term antiemetic or vestibular suppressant for severe symptoms, with attention to sedation.
- [1 marks] Assess dehydration and provide oral or intravenous fluid replacement as tolerated, monitoring clinical response.

b) Explain TWO problems with the proposed month-long medication and bed-rest plan. [2 marks]
- [1 marks] Prolonged suppression can impede vestibular compensation and cause sedation/falls.
- [1 marks] Prolonged bed rest delays recovery; encourage safe early mobilisation and vestibular rehabilitation once vomiting permits.

c) Give TWO criteria you would assess before considering discharge. [2 marks]
- [1 marks] Can hydrate orally and symptoms are controlled sufficiently for safe basic activity.
- [1 marks] Can mobilise safely with an appropriate home/support plan and clear reassessment advice; diagnostic confidence must remain adequate.


QUESTION 4 (5 marks)

a) Explain why a unilateral vestibular disturbance can cause both nystagmus and nausea. [2 marks]
- [1 marks] Asymmetric vestibular input is interpreted as movement and drives compensatory slow eye movement with corrective fast phases.
- [1 marks] Conflicting vestibular, visual and proprioceptive information activates autonomic/emetic pathways.

b) If Daniel also developed acute unilateral hearing loss, how would this alter your differential and the urgency of assessment? [2 marks]
- [1 marks] Consider cochlear/inner-ear disease such as labyrinthitis or sudden sensorineural hearing loss rather than isolated neuritis.
- [1 marks] Also consider vascular inner-ear/brainstem involvement; new hearing loss requires urgent reassessment and must not be assumed to prove a benign peripheral cause.

c) Why should the same bedside eye-movement algorithm not be used to rule out stroke in a patient with brief attacks who is now asymptomatic? [1 marks]
- [1 marks] HINTS-type interpretation applies to an appropriate ongoing acute vestibular syndrome and requires training; performance outside that setting is not a reliable exclusion test.


COMMON REASONING ERRORS
- Calling a recent cold proof of a benign cause.
- Using HINTS in intermittent dizziness, without appropriate training, or as a substitute for an overall examination.
- Requiring steroids as an essential answer; their benefit is uncertain.

CLINICAL THINKING TO TAKE INTO THE NEXT PATIENT
- Build a syndrome from duration, auditory symptoms, gait and eye findings before attaching a label.
- Ask what makes home care safe, rather than treating reduced nausea as the endpoint.
